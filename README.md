The objective of this component of the project is to calibrate a spatial kernel that can be scaled up to global resolution for later use in metapopulation modeling. We begin with the US due to the abundance of high-quality origin-destination pair data. We ultimately fit a lognormal distribution, fit on Meta Movement Distribution observations, for each county in the US.

The two sources of data in use are derived from Veraset, which provides device-level observations of mobility across the US. We use observations from June-2025 for Veraset and June-2026 for Meta. Devices are coded to IDs and are pinged whenever they visit a SafeGraph point of interest, of which there are about two million in the US. Veraset reports no time lag between a device arriving and being recorded at a particular location. Geographic location is determined by the Census Block Group in which a device is found (CBG). GEOHASH-5s are also available, but have relatively low granularity and thus produce an inflated count of short-range movement. For the purposes of comparison, we also reject counties for which fewer than 10,000 devices were recorded as they produced non-monotonically decreasing estimates of mobility, which is at odds with literature estimates of movement. 

Meta computes the spatial kernel on a per-county basis in the US (admin-2 globally), by first sampling Meta product users in the evening to determine their home / point-of-origin, then sampling them again at a random time of the day. From, this they assemble a four bin movement distribution that describes where users are located: 0 (home tile), 0-10 km, 10-100 km, 100 km+. For comparison to Veraset, we sample locations proportionally to their residence time in each location (e.g. a user who spent 10 minutes at location A and 10 minutes at location B has a 50% chance of being allocated to A or B on that day). 

However, Meta only provides a distribution over distances; it offers no visibility on allocation. Akin to gravity models, we make allocation decisions by constructing a joint distribution over population and distance, using census-derived population data to determine per-cell population. We then take expectations over that distribution, assuming our distribution describes all members of each county. Similarly, we assume Veraset observations represent whole-population movement and so scale observations to the population level.

After comparing it to other candidate distributions, we construct a lognormal spatial distribution according to Meta and compare it to Veraset in a few ways: Common Parts of Commuters (CPC), total variation per band (TV), and leave-one-out cross validation. We generate comparison figures and maps as well.
